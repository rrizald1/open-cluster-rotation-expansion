About this study

Open clusters evolve dynamically under the influence of internal processes and the galactic tidal field, producing signatures such as rotation, expansion, contraction, and extended tidal tails. Studies of cluster rotation have largely focused on the core, with few extending the analysis to tidal tails, while expansion and contraction signatures remain less explored.

This study investigates the signatures of rotation and expansion from cluster cores to tidal tails using the open cluster sample and tidal-tail membership identified by Risbud et al. (2025). The goal is to explore how these kinematic signatures vary between the core and tails and provide observational insight into the dynamical evolution of open clusters.

Methods

Data & sample

Tidal-tail and core membership are adopted from Risbud et al. (2025), based on a model-independent Convergent-Point method. Astrometric data are supplemented with Gaia DR3.

Radial velocities are primarily taken from Gaia DR3, with missing measurements supplemented by the Survey of Surveys (SoS; Tsantaki et al., 2022).

For each cluster, three samples are analyzed in parallel: Core+Tails, Core-only, and Tails-only, allowing kinematic signatures to be traced to the core, tails, or both.

Projection-effect correction

A cluster's bulk motion can mimic internal expansion or rotation. These perspective effects are removed before measuring intrinsic kinematic signatures, following the formulations of van Leeuwen (2009).

The expected motion induced by the cluster's bulk velocity is calculated for each star and subtracted from its observed radial velocity and proper motion.

Detecting rotation — radial velocity

The 25% of stars closest to the cluster center are excluded to minimize the impact of the high central velocity dispersion. For each position angle (PA), the cluster is divided into two sides and the mean radial velocity difference, ΔV, between them is calculated. The PA that maximizes ΔV defines the orientation of the cluster's rotation axis.

The rotation component is then obtained from the slope of the corrected radial velocity against XR, the projected distance of each star from the rotation axis:

$$RV_{corr} = v\cos i \cdot X_R$$

where v cos i is the rotation component projected along the line of sight.

Detecting rotation, expansion & contraction — proper motion

On the plane of the sky, rotation shows up as the tangential proper-motion component, μT, increasing with radial distance R from the center:

$$\mu_T = v\sin i \cdot R$$

where v sin i is the rotation component projected onto the plane of the sky.

Expansion and contraction are traced the same way through the radial component, μR: a positive trend means the cluster is expanding, a negative trend means it is contracting.

$$\mu_R = \chi \cdot R$$

χ > 0 → expansion · χ < 0 → contraction

Statistical significance

We adopt the statistical tests and significance criteria from Jadhav et al. (2024).

For the RV-based analysis, a two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) test is used to assess whether the two sides of the cluster have significantly different radial-velocity distributions.

For the PM-based analysis, Spearman rank correlation is used to test for monotonic trends between the kinematic components and stellar radius. Measurement uncertainties are propagated through 10,000 Monte Carlo realizations, with the median correlation coefficient and p-value adopted as the final estimates.

A trend is classified as strong & significant when median |ρ| ≥ 0.4 and median p ≤ 0.05, and as significant but weak when median |ρ| < 0.4 with median p ≤ 0.05. For both RV- and PM-based fits, the slope must also exceed its 1σ uncertainty.

Key Findings

18 / 19clusters show a different rotation axis in core vs. tail
Tails > CoreRotation and expansion are more easily detected in tidal tails
8 / 19clusters show significant tail contraction

The rotation axis twists from core to tail

In 18 of the 19 clusters, the best-fit position angle measured in the tidal tails differs from that of the core, indicating a systematic twist in the cluster's rotation axis. This pattern is consistent with the influence of the galactic tidal field as stars move from the cluster core into the tidal tails (Jadhav et al., 2024, 2025; Bissekenov et al., 2025).

Rotation and expansion/contraction are both stronger in the tails

Across all three tests, RV-based rotation, PM-based rotation, and PM-based expansion/contraction, kinematic signatures are detected more frequently in the Tails-only component than in the Core-only component. This suggests that rotation and expansion/contraction are more easily detected in the tidal tails, where the kinematic signal may be less affected by the high velocity dispersion and crowding of the cluster core.

8 of 19 clusters show tail contraction

Eight clusters, NGC 6633, Alessi 3, Alessi 9, Blanco 1, Roslund 6, RSG 4, Ruprecht 147, and Stock 2, show statistically significant contraction in their Tails-only component. Although stars in tidal tails are dynamically unbound and would generally be expected to move away from the cluster over time, their motion is not necessarily purely radial. One possible explanation is epicyclic motion, in which stars moving through the galactic tidal field can experience phases where their separation from the cluster temporarily decreases. This could produce an apparent contraction even though the stars remain unbound.
